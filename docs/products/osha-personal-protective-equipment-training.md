## OSHA Personal Protective Equipment (PPE) Training

This OSHA Personal Protective Equipment (PPE) Training course is developed in alignment with OSHA’s General Industry Standard **29 CFR 1910 Subpart I** and Construction Industry Standard **29 CFR 1926 Subpart E**. The course prepares employees, supervisors, and safety personnel to recognize workplace hazards, select appropriate PPE, and use protective gear correctly to prevent injuries and occupational illnesses.

Participants will explore the different types of PPE—ranging from head, eye, and face protection to respiratory devices, protective footwear, hearing protection, fall protection, and chemical-resistant gear. The course also emphasizes proper inspection, maintenance, and storage practices to keep PPE functioning the way OSHA intends. By the end of the course, learners understand how to create safer work environments and comply with OSHA’s mandatory PPE requirements.

### Why OSHA PPE Training Is Important

PPE serves as the last line of defense against workplace hazards. Even with strong administrative controls and engineering safeguards, workers may still be exposed to physical, chemical, biological, and mechanical risks. This course ensures employees understand:

- How to identify hazards that require PPE
- The correct PPE for specific job tasks
- How to properly don, doff, and adjust PPE
- Limitations of PPE and when additional controls are needed
- Employee and employer responsibilities under OSHA regulations
- How to maintain, clean, and store PPE to extend its lifespan
- The importance of hazard assessments and written PPE programs

### Who Can Benefit from OSHA Personal Protective Equipment Training?

This training is essential for anyone working in environments where exposure to hazards is possible. PPE is widely used across multiple industries to reduce injuries from chemicals, noise, falling objects, sharp tools, extreme temperatures, biohazards, and more. Individuals who benefit from this course include:

- Manufacturing workers handling machinery, chemicals, or high-noise environments
- Construction workers exposed to heights, dust, debris, and falling objects
- Healthcare workers involved in patient care, biohazard exposure, or sanitation
- Laboratory technicians working with chemicals, biological samples, or radiation
- Food service workers handling hot surfaces, cleaning agents, and sharp equipment
- Firefighters responding to hazardous environments and emergency incidents
- Paramedics and first responders dealing with unpredictable exposure risks
- Military personnel operating in diverse and high-risk environments
- Oil and gas workers exposed to flammable materials and heavy machinery
- Agriculture workers handling pesticides, livestock, machinery, and outdoor hazards
- Warehouse and logistics staff exposed to moving equipment and manual handling risks
- Facility maintenance teams working with electrical, chemical, and mechanical hazards
